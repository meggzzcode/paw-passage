You booked the cabin with the view, and then you saw the photo of the staircase to the only bedroom. Your fourteen-year-old dog hesitates at two steps at home. Much senior-dog travel advice talks about the car; this guide is about the rental, because a place you cannot change is where an older dog's trip is won or lost.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. Rules vary by place and host.

We label how sure we are: **Confirmed**, **What most people report** and **Unknown**. Where we quote forums, the sample is small and not random, and many threads are from host or landlord forums (details near the end).

## Do this first

**Bottom line:** talk to your vet, get photos and written answers from the host, and only then pay.

1. **Call your vet** before booking and ask whether the trip suits your dog.
2. **Send the host the message below** and ask for photos of the route, steps and floors.
3. **Pack for slipping and accidents:** non-slip rugs, your dog's own bed, washable layers and your vet's medication list.

## The short answer

**Bottom line:** the rental matters more than the road, and almost every answer you need comes from a vet or the host, in writing.

- Ask your vet whether the trip suits your dog before you book. We found no age cutoff in the sources we read.
- Check the listing for stairs, floors, thresholds, bed and couch height and steps to the yard, and ask the host for photos. The check list is below.
- Four portable fixes come up across vet sources: non-slip rugs or mats, short nails, grip aids, and ramps with traction. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs) [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- Manager rules can clash with an older dog's needs, such as furniture rules, unattended rules and weight or count limits. Ask in writing.
- If your dog leaks or may have accidents, bring washable or waterproof layers, report any accident and ask the host how accidents are handled. See the accidents section.
- We found no vet source that gives a stop interval, a distance to a vet or a heat limit. Ask your vet for your dog's numbers.

## Can you take a senior dog to a vacation rental?

**Bottom line:** often yes, with your vet's okay and a rental that suits your dog's body.

Often, yes, if your vet agrees the trip suits your dog and the rental suits your dog's mobility. We found no age rule in the sources we read, so the decision rests on your dog's health, mobility and car comfort, and your vet is the first stop, before the booking.

Forum threads show the dilemma, but they are anecdotes, not evidence. One owner on a forum described a nine-year-old Lab with hip dysplasia and a booked third-floor rental with six flights and no elevator; the only reply suggested boarding the dog, hiring a strong person to carry him, or asking about paying extra for a ground-floor unit. [[18]](https://thelabradorforum.com/posts/390587) We do not know what happened next. The lesson is about timing: the problem was found after booking.

| Situation | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Your vet says the trip is fine and the dog moves comfortably | Go, and check the rental carefully |
| Your dog is stiff, slow to rise or hesitates at stairs | Ask your vet first; these are listed signs of joint disease [[5]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis) |
| Your dog is newly confused, restless at night or having accidents | Ask your vet first; see the section on behavior changes |
| Your vet advises against travel | Consider a sitter or boarding, and ask what to look for |

## What should you check on the listing?

**Bottom line:** stairs and floors decide most trips, and a listing rarely tells you enough.

Check six things: stairs and floors, thresholds, the route from parking to the door, bed and couch height, steps to the yard, and quiet. A listing rarely states all of these, so the answer is usually photos and a message to the host, not the listing text.

- [ ] **Stairs.** Where is the bedroom, and how many flights between the entrance, the bedroom and the bathroom? Also ask whether the steps are open-backed, narrow or steep, and whether the treads are bare or carpeted (see the next section).
- [ ] **Floors.** Hardwood and tile can be slippery for an older dog. Dog Aging Project says to limit an older dog's access to slippery hard floors, and Banfield suggests non-slip rugs or yoga mats on them. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs) [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home)
- [ ] **Thresholds and door sills** at the entrance and to the yard.
- [ ] **Route from where you park to the door.** Steps, curbs, a slope.
- [ ] **Bed and couch height.** Cornell lists trouble getting onto couches as a sign, and suggests ramps for beds, couches or cars. [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- [ ] **Steps down to the yard,** and whether it is fenced (see our [fenced-yard guide](/guides/vacation-rental-secure-fenced-yard)).
- [ ] **Quiet.** Ask about neighbors and road noise if your dog sleeps poorly in new places. We did not find a source that sets a standard, so this is your judgment.

### A borrowed photo standard

Airbnb has a published standard for how hosts photograph step-free features. It says to show no steps, stairs or curbs over 2 inches along the entrance route, with photos every 10 feet and thresholds shown close up. [[1]](https://www.airbnb.com/resources/hosting-homes/a/a-guide-to-photographing-accessibility-features-30) It also lists "Step-free access" and "Step-free bedroom access" among its home accessibility features, and says guests can choose accessibility features in the Filters menu. [[2]](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3138)

Important caveat: these are built for human guests. Whether they describe what is easy for a dog is our inference, not something Airbnb says. Step-free access to the entrance is plausibly relevant to a dog, but the standard has no flooring or bed-height photos. Use it as a model for what to ask for, not as a guarantee.

## What should you ask the host?

**Bottom line:** specific photos and written answers beat a vague "is it accessible?".

Ask for specific photos and written answers before you pay. The point is that a vague "is it accessible?" gets a vague answer, while "send a photo of the steps from the driveway" gets a fact.

Photos to request:

- The route from parking to the front door, with any steps or curbs
- The entrance threshold, close up
- The stairs to the bedroom and bathroom, close up from the side and the front, so you can see whether they are open-backed, narrow or steep, and the flooring on them
- The main floor flooring, and the bedroom floor
- The path to the yard and the yard's slope

A message you can copy:

> Hi [Host], we are considering [dates] with our [age]-year-old [breed]. She is steady but slow on stairs and slippery floors. Could you tell me: (1) Is there a step-free route from parking to the door, and could you send a photo? (2) What flooring is in the living area and bedroom, and are the stairs carpeted or bare? (3) Are there stairs between the bedroom, bathroom and entrance? (4) Are there steps to the yard? (5) May we put down a rubber-backed rug or a small ramp, and bring our own bed and washable bedding? (6) How are accidents by an older dog handled, and is any deposit involved? We will clean up and tell you right away. Thank you.

If your dog may leak, you could add a line saying so; no source we read says whether hosts want to know in advance, so that is your call (see the accidents section). Send it through the booking platform so the answers are on record. Our [pet-friendly rental checklist](/guides/pet-friendly-vacation-rental-checklist) covers the general questions, and the [pet cost tool](/tools/pet-cost-check) helps you confirm the total.

## How do you make slippery floors safer in a rental you cannot change?

**Bottom line:** bring grip with you, ask before you lay anything down, and expect hard floors.

Four fixes appear across vet sources: non-slip rugs or mats, short nails, grip aids and ramps with traction. They are portable, so you can bring them, but ask the host before you move anything.

- **Rugs or mats.** Banfield suggests non-slip surfaces such as rugs or yoga mats. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) Dog Aging Project suggests them where the dog lies down and in high-traffic areas, and says to be present the first time your dog uses them. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Short nails.** Dog Aging Project says nail length matters for paw-pad traction. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Grip aids.** Boots, socks with rubber soles and toe grips can help; the same page cautions about toe grips for chewers. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Ramps and stairs for furniture or a car.** AKC says to match the height to the sofa, bed or trunk, check the weight limit, and look for a non-slip surface. [[7]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/general-health/dog-stairs-and-ramps/) Banfield says ramps need the right incline and traction. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) We found no authoritative slope number, and none of these pages says a ramp works for a full indoor flight of stairs.

Banfield also lists an orthopedic bed with a low profile, a washable cover and a non-slip bottom. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) Bring your own bed so there is something familiar and the host's furniture stays untouched.

**What most people report (level 2, small sample).** Four of the 17 forum threads we read for this guide point to traction on stairs or floors: rubber bath mats cut to fit the steps, traction strips, traction on bare treads, and carpet laid for footing. [[19]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/looking-for-advice-on-wooden-steps/) [[20]](https://tripawds.com/forums/size-and-age-matters/senior-stair-struggles) [[21]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/stairs-was-fine-and-then-it-changed) [[22]](https://forums.mst3k.com/t/older-dogs-and-stairs/20432) Three of the four are from one forum, Tripawds, about three-legged dogs, so they are not fully independent. It matches the vet-source advice above; it is not extra proof.

One host on a host forum wrote that hard floors are far better than carpets and rugs. [[23]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/pet-friendly-hosts-tips-for-setting-up-your-place/18610) That is a single report, but it fits our inference, not a source: hosts who welcome dogs may choose floors that are easy to clean, which are the floors vets flag as slippery. So plan for hard floors even if the listing photos do not show them.

Do not assume you can carry a large dog up stairs. On Tripawds, one thread concluded that carrying was out for a roughly 50 lb dog on a long staircase, and in another an owner said their dog growled when she tried to carry him. [[19]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/looking-for-advice-on-wooden-steps/) [[21]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/stairs-was-fine-and-then-it-changed) Those are two single reports, not a rule, and we found no vet source on carrying. One owner on another forum suggested a sling and a ramp for a couple of front steps, and mentioned a dog that hated ramps. [[22]](https://forums.mst3k.com/t/older-dogs-and-stairs/20432) It is also a safety question for you.

## What if your dog balks at the stairs when you arrive?

**Bottom line:** do not force it; stop, find a plan with the host, and call your vet if it is new.

This is where the check list pays off, because a dog who refuses stairs on day one leaves you few choices. Forum posts we read are single reports. On Tripawds, an owner whose dog refused the stairs of her apartment building pulled him up by the leash, and a reply warned this "could definitely turn him off" the stairs. [[21]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/stairs-was-fine-and-then-it-changed) In the same thread a reply said many dogs, especially three-legged ones, dislike open-back stairs. In another thread, an 11-year-old, 80 lb dog fell back down from about the fourth step on narrow, steep, open-backed stairs. [[20]](https://tripawds.com/forums/size-and-age-matters/senior-stair-struggles) Both threads are from one forum, so we treat them as anecdotes.

What we can say from the vet sources above: hesitating at stairs is a listed sign of joint disease, so a new refusal is a reason to call your vet. [[5]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis) Our suggestion, not a vet's: before you travel, ask the host to move you to another unit or cancel under the booking terms if the stairs turn out to be a problem, which is another reason to get the answers in writing.

## What if your dog leaks or has accidents?

**Bottom line:** tell your vet, pack washable layers, report any accident and ask how the host handles it.

Leaking in an older dog can have causes a vet should check, so start there. In one UK forum thread a poster said her vet was not surprised her older dog was leaking, and another poster suggested taking a urine sample to the vet. [[27]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/1128953-geriatric-dog-peeing-in-house) That is forum talk, not a diagnosis.

**What most people report (level 2, small sample).** Three of the 17 forum threads we read mention incontinence or leaking in older dogs as something owners and hosts deal with: a host who had guests arrive with pads for geriatric dogs, an owner thread where one person had no trips away because the dog was doubly incontinent and the original poster listed the difficulty of finding someone willing to care for an elderly dog so they could go away among their frustrations, and the thread above. [[25]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/instant-book-and-pets/45138) [[26]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/5090152-old-dog-support-thread?page=1) [[27]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/1128953-geriatric-dog-peeing-in-house) Two of the three are from Mumsnet, a UK forum, and the host post is a passing remark. It shows the issue is common enough that hosts have seen it, not how hosts react to it.

**What most people report (level 2, small sample).** Three of the 17 threads, all from one host forum, Airhosts, describe hosts who plan for accidents: one keeps a cleaner and old towels on hand, one has a house rule asking guests to report any accident, and one host said about 99% of guests grab a bag or a mop. [[23]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/pet-friendly-hosts-tips-for-setting-up-your-place/18610) [[24]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/are-these-reasonable-rules-for-dogs/24289) [[25]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/instant-book-and-pets/45138) These are hosts' own views, so they say what hosts do or want, not what a platform requires. It lines up with Brindley Beach's page, which says its pet fee does not cover unreported accidents. [[13]](https://www.brindleybeach.com/node/3691)

Practical steps, mostly our suggestions:

- **Layers.** Bring washable or waterproof bedding, pads and a sheet or blanket for furniture. Three of the 17 threads mention owners bringing their own blanket or hosts supplying throws. [[24]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/are-these-reasonable-rules-for-dogs/24289) [[28]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/uk/3413892-Question-about-a-holiday-cottage) [[30]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/renting-to-dog-owners-your-thoughts/3126?page=2) One poster recommended a vet bed because it locks urine in; that is a forum suggestion, not a vet's. [[27]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/1128953-geriatric-dog-peeing-in-house)
- **Report and clean right away.** Tell the host or manager and say what you did.
- **Tell the host?** We found no source that says whether hosts want advance notice of incontinence, or refuse bookings because of it. It is an unknown; the message above lets you decide.
- **Smell and stains.** Two threads from property owners say dog urine smell or stains can be hard to remove: one said "it will leave permanent dark spots" and the other said replacing carpet had "zero effect." [[31]](https://biggerpockets.com/forums/52/topics/838571-can-i-save-old-dog-peed-on-wood-floors) [[32]](https://www.biggerpockets.com/forums/52/topics/839990-dog-urine-and-crazy-repair-bill-what-to-do) A host wrote that a spot that gets marked gets marked again, and guests in a UK thread disliked a cottage that smelled of dog, though neither is about urine smell or stains. [[23]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/pet-friendly-hosts-tips-for-setting-up-your-place/18610) [[28]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/uk/3413892-Question-about-a-holiday-cottage) Only two items fit, and both are long-term property owners, not holiday hosts, so this is below our three-item bar and stays a pair of single reports. It is our reason to suggest reporting an accident early.
- **Cleaners.** One poster in a UK thread said ammonia cleaners can encourage a dog to mark the same spot. [[27]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/1128953-geriatric-dog-peeing-in-house) We found no vet or manufacturer source, so we do not recommend a cleaner. Ask your vet.

If a host later asks for money after the stay, our [damage-charge guide](/guides/airbnb-pet-damage-charge-after-checkout) covers the clocks and what to reply. Whether a pet fee, a deposit or a charge applies to an accident you reported and cleaned is unknown: ask the host in writing, and run the [pet cost check](/tools/pet-cost-check) to see what you would pay in total.


## How far should the rental be from a vet?

**Bottom line:** no source sets a distance, so pick your own limit and check the map before you book.

We could not confirm any distance. No source we read sets one. ASPCA says to save a local veterinary emergency hospital at your destination, and its poison control number, in your phone. [[11]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts)

A practical approach: decide how many minutes you would accept, then check on a map for the nearest emergency vet to the rental address, confirm its hours and whether it is open around the clock, and save the number before you leave. That is our suggestion, not a standard.

For poisoning, ASPCA Animal Poison Control is 888-426-4435 [[33]](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control) and Pet Poison Helpline is 855-764-7661 [[34]](https://www.petpoisonhelpline.com/); a consultation fee may apply. Save both with the vet's number.

Also think about who decides if your dog becomes seriously ill while you are away. On a TouringPlans forum, one owner's 13-year-old dog needed an emergency vet on a trip, and the person who took the dog to the clinic would not let them do anything without the owner deciding by phone. [[29]](https://forum.touringplans.com/t/vacation-help-trigger-warning-dog-died/88310?page=2) That is one painful anecdote, not a pattern. Our suggestion, which we could not source: ask your vet what to write down for a carer, and keep your phone reachable.

## What should you pack and carry?

**Bottom line:** records, medication, ID, your dog's own bed and a way to handle slipping and accidents.

ASPCA says to carry a copy of medical records if your dog's history is complicated, to put visible ID on the collar and consider a microchip, and to check for an interstate health certificate. [[11]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts) We could not read AVMA's travel page, so we are not stating certificate time windows; ask your vet and the destination state.

- [ ] Records, and a medication list written by your vet
- [ ] Medications, with extra in case the return is delayed
- [ ] Collar with ID tag, and microchip details (see our [ID tag guide](/guides/dog-id-tags-and-gps-trackers-for-travel-guide))
- [ ] Your dog's own bed, non-slip rugs or mats, and any ramp
- [ ] Accident kit: towels, paper towels, waste bags, waterproof pads or washable layers if your dog may leak, and your own sheet or blanket for furniture
- [ ] The nearest emergency vet and its number saved offline
- [ ] A [first-aid kit](/guides/dog-travel-first-aid-kit-guide) and our [cabin packing list](/guides/dog-friendly-cabin-packing-list)

## What about the car and rest stops?

**Bottom line:** no vet source gives a stop interval, so ask your vet and watch your dog.

We found no vet source that gives a stop interval. Some forum replies and commercial blogs give numbers, but we could not trace them to a vet, so we are not repeating them. Ask your vet what suits your dog and watch your dog for discomfort.

**What most people do (level 2, small sample).** We read nine forum threads and owner posts, mostly UK and mostly not about rentals. Three of the nine (one forum thread and two owner posts) mention frequent stops or a slower pace, and three mention shade or heat as a constraint. The threads disagree on the details, and none gives an interval a vet backs, so treat this as a description of what owners say, not advice. The interval itself stays unknown: ask your vet.

On heat, we found no vet threshold either. ASPCA says never to leave a pet in a parked car. [[11]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts) Some forum replies say a short wait is fine when it is cool; that is anecdote, and the ASPCA line is the safer one for an older dog. Plan stops where someone can stay with your dog, and see our [car safety guide](/guides/dog-car-safety-harness-and-crate-guide). Anything to calm your dog for the drive is a question for your vet.

## When should you ask your vet first?

**Bottom line:** call before booking for any sign below, and again if it appears on the trip.

Ask your vet before booking if your dog shows any sign below, and again if it appears during the trip. We do not give medical advice here; this list is a prompt to call.

- **Joint signs.** Banfield and Cornell list stiffness, trouble rising, hesitation on stairs and difficulty getting onto couches or other raised surfaces, and Banfield says not to dismiss them as just old age. [[5]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- **Behavior changes.** ASPCA lists confusion, changes in sleep, activity or house training and says to report all changes to your vet. [[10]](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/behavior-problems-older-dogs) AKC says a full evaluation by your vet is imperative. [[9]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/recognizing-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
- **Do not assume dementia.** Dog Aging Project says pain, dental disease, sensory loss and other illness can cause behavior changes. [[8]](https://dogagingproject.org/understanding-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
- **Leaking or new accidents.** Forum posters say it is common in older dogs, and one said a vet was not surprised. [[27]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/1128953-geriatric-dog-peeing-in-house) We found no vet source we read for this guide, so ask yours.
- **Routine.** The same page says older dogs have a much tougher time with inconsistencies in routine and that a consistent routine is key; keeping feeding, walks and bedtime as close to normal as you can is our suggestion. [[8]](https://dogagingproject.org/understanding-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)

## How we label certainty

**Bottom line:** Confirmed is the strongest label, and Unknown means nothing we read settles it.

**Confirmed** means a vet, platform or manager page says it, with a citation. **What most people report** means a pattern across at least three items we read, always with the count: a small, non-random sample, never a rule. We read 17 forum threads for the rental topics and, separately, nine forum threads and owner posts about travel and the car. Many of the 17 are from host or landlord forums (Airhosts, BiggerPockets), many are UK or years old, and several come from one forum, so we say so each time. **Unknown** means nothing we read settles it, and we say who to ask. Anything from only one or two items is written as a single report.

## Before you book

**Bottom line:** run this list before you pay.

- [ ] Vet has okayed the trip
- [ ] Photos received: route, threshold, stairs, floors, yard
- [ ] Written answer on rugs, ramp and your own bed
- [ ] Stairs checked: count, open-backed or not, steepness, bare or carpeted
- [ ] Waterproof or washable layers packed if your dog may leak
- [ ] Written answer on accidents, unattended rules and weight or count limits
- [ ] Total price confirmed with the [pet cost tool](/tools/pet-cost-check)
- [ ] Emergency vet saved, with its hours
- [ ] Someone reachable, and your vet's advice on what to leave for a carer
- [ ] Cancellation terms saved; use the [rental checklist](/tools/checklist)
