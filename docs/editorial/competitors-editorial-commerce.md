## Per-site findings

### NBC Select (2 articles)
- Structure: buying guide. Carriers piece is about 3,500+ words: "Our top picks" (7, expander for 4 more), "How I picked", 14 numbered picks, "How to shop", FAQ, calming tips, "Meet our experts", "Why trust NBC Select?". Harness piece is about 2,000 words with 3 top picks (carrier, crate, harness) and "Skip ahead" jump links.
- Intro: short relatable hook, then immediate statement that vets and safety experts informed the picks.
- Rhythm: descriptive H2s phrased as reader questions, H3 per product. Each pick: category label ("Best tote carrier"), photo, "What we like" / "Something to note" bullets, narrative review, bold spec line (pet weight, dimensions, material).
- TOC: "In this article" anchors, repeated in sidebar and at the end (carriers piece).
- Images: Getty or staff-credited hero, product thumbnails, staff-with-their-dog photos (strong authenticity cue). Many images reportedly have empty alt text.
- Sources: named experts with credentials, Center for Pet Safety, AVMA, USDA, IATA, airline policy pages. Brand claims are attributed to "the brand".
- Tone: friendly, first-person staff anecdotes, reassuring.
- Disclosure: above the headline, small line. Quote: "When you click on or buy through our links, we earn a commission." plus "Learn more" link and an independence statement.
- Buy links: text price links "$X at Amazon / Chewy / Petco", often several retailers, sale prices struck through, sometimes long tracking URLs. Sidebar "Deal of the Day".
- Trust: named experts, "without undisclosed financial conflicts" statement, staff testing photos, award badges.
- Friction: "$undefined" placeholders in top-picks list, missing spec fields, headline year (2026) vs timestamp (2024), byline-title mismatch, heavy chrome and ad widgets around ~2,000-3,500 words.

### The Points Guy (3 articles)
- Structure: 8-10 minute guides. No TOC. H2s are question-based ("Which dog breeds can fly?", "Weight limits for flying with dogs"). Ends with "Bottom line".
- Intro: first-person anecdote or empathy hook ("daunting and stressful").
- Quick answer: collapsible "Quick summary" box ("Show summary" toggle), labelled as generated by AI with editorial support, with "What to consider" and "What you'll miss". Not an "our pick" box.
- Images: Getty stock plus captioned staff dog photos with photographer credit (authentic).
- Sources: mostly inline links to airline policies and pet-transport vendors; one named vet; no regulators in one piece.
- Tone: conversational, light humour (the "em-barking" pun), directive ("never", "always").
- Disclosure: under byline, above comments, but it is the credit-card disclosure. Quote: "The cards we feature here are from partners who compensate us when you are approved through our site." Repeated in footer "Our commitment to transparency", linked to advertising policy and review methodology.
- Monetisation: credit-card tiles, "Apply now", CardMatch inside a pet article; a topic mismatch readers can feel.
- Friction: 2020 and 2022 articles with no visible "updated" date while the footer and related stories read 2026; policy and fee claims without links to primary airline pages; mega-menu and ad density.

## What makes them feel professional and authentic to a 25-35 reader
1. Named humans with their own dogs in photos and first-person anecdotes (both sites). Reads as lived experience.
2. Expert roster with credentials and an independent safety body (CPS) rather than only brand claims.
3. Skimmable, question-phrased headings and a TOC or "skip ahead" links.
4. Pick cards with a consistent spec line and a "something to note" downside.
5. Plain, small, early disclosure line with a "Learn more" link.
6. Light humour that does not undercut safety advice.

## Friction seen
- Stale dates and mismatched year labels (both sites): the single biggest credibility leak.
- Ad, newsletter and deal widgets crowding the reading column.
- Disclosure sits above the fold but is tiny and easily skipped (NBC); TPG's refers to credit cards, not pet products.
- Price links through long tracking URLs and variable prices that go stale.
- Empty alt text and placeholder bugs.
- AI-generated summary labelled but unreviewed-looking.
- TPG: no TOC on long guides.

## Head-to-head: Paws & Passage vs the two readable competitors
Source: the two P&P guides above (read via summariser).

Where P&P is already ahead:
- Disclosure wording and placement: under the hero, before the TOC, and repeated on every card and the footer. Plain wording including "It does not change what we write" (bowls guide). Stronger and more honest than NBC's single small line or TPG's card-only disclosure.
- Visible "Last reviewed Nov 19, 2026", source count, access dates per source: beats both competitors on freshness.
- Numbered inline citations to a source list (CPS, CDC, ASPCA, makers) versus competitors' loose links.
- Explicit "not tested by us" and "How sure we are" sections. Rare and credible.
- Two tables, one listing what could not be confirmed; "not stated" instead of guessing.
- TOC at top and again near the end; "quick answer" with bold lead-ins; FAQ.
- No prices or ratings, so nothing goes stale.

Where P&P is behind:
- No first-hand testing and no photos of real people and dogs. The "not tested" honesty is good but the lived-experience warmth competitors get from staff-dog photos is missing. One hero image per guide only.
- No named expert or vet reviewer visible in what was read, so the trust load rests on documents, not people. (Unverified: summariser may have omitted a byline detail.)
- No "Our pick" box or three-picks-at-a-glance at the top; buyers must scan cards.
- Two buttons per card (Amazon and details) is clean, but there is no per-card spec line with weight/size at a glance comparable to NBC's.
- Dual FAQ (accordion plus static) looks duplicated.
- Tone is careful and cautious to the point of reading like a research memo; less personality than competitors for a 25-35 audience.
- Wide comparison tables likely cramped on phones (inferred, not verified from rendered HTML).
- Honest limit: P&P's own self-descriptions are my reading of a summary, not a rendered-page audit.

## Patterns for Paws & Passage to adapt
1. "What to buy first" block directly under the quick answer: 3 items max (e.g. harness or crate, water, bowl), each one line, spec chip, one button. Terracotta border/left rule, matching the spec cards.
2. Pick label chips ("Best for small dogs", "Best for crates") on each spec card, with a "Something to note" line taken from NBC's pattern. It gives a visible downside and keeps the tone honest.
3. Spec line as a fixed row on every card (size range, weight range, tested by CPS: yes/no/not stated). Same fields every time.
4. Add real faces: author byline with a real dog, a "tested on my dog" label only where true, and keep "not tested" where false. Personal micro-captions with the dog's name and trip.
5. Named reviewer (vet or certified trainer) with credentials in a "Who checked this" strip. Do not invent one.
6. One-sentence disclosure at top in plain words plus "Learn more", keep the per-card "Affiliate link" tag.
7. Question-phrased H2s ("Can my dog ride in a crate in the back seat?") for skimming and search.
8. Mobile: collapse wide tables into stacked cards under ~600px; sticky mini-TOC or "Jump to" chips; single-column buttons with 44px targets.
9. Light humour in one or two spots (TPG style), never in safety rules.
10. "Updated" date plus a short change-log line ("Nov 19: swapped crate pick after maker changed spec").

## Patterns to avoid copying
- Credit-card or deal widgets and sidebars unrelated to pets (TPG); "Deal of the Day" urgency (NBC).
- AI-generated summary box presented near the top; P&P's human-written quick answer is better.
- Undated or mismatched dates; headline-year inflation.
- Disclosure only as a tiny line above the headline, or one that talks about something else.
- Showing live prices and strikethrough sale prices that go stale; long tracking URLs.
- Placeholder text, blank spec fields, empty alt text.
- Quoting brand claims as fact; keep "according to the maker".
- Stock photography dressed up as first-hand experience; any "tested" claim not backed by real testing.
- Duplicated FAQ blocks.
