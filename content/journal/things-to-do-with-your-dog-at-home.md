Some days the plan is simply "home." The laundry is on, the weather is fine, and your dog is looking at you as if you might have an idea. You probably do. Most dogs do not need a big production. They need you to pay attention for a few minutes in a way that is a little different from yesterday.

## The short answer

The easiest things to do with your dog at home are short, calm and nose-led: a sniff trail, a small search game, a new trick, a change of scenery inside the house. Five to fifteen minutes is plenty. Pick one, watch how your dog responds, and stop while they are still interested.

## Six ideas to try

**1. A sniff trail.** Let your dog watch you place a few small, dog-safe treats along one wall, then say "find it" and follow at their pace. Make it a little harder another day by moving the trail to another room.

**2. Hide-and-seek, dog edition.** Hide somewhere easy, call once, and celebrate when they find you. Let them succeed quickly at first. If your dog is shy of the game, skip it.

**3. Trick of the week.** Choose one silly trick, such as spin or touch-my-hand, and practice it in tiny sessions. Quit on a success, even a small one.

**4. A recycling-bin puzzle.** Put a few treats inside a cardboard roll or a small box, fold the ends closed and let your dog work it out. ASPCA's DIY enrichment guide lists several household puzzles like this. Stay close and take away anything your dog tries to swallow.

**5. A room tour.** Bring something new into a familiar room, such as a basket, a folded towel that smells of somewhere else, or a bag of clean leaves, and let your dog investigate it. A little novelty is its own game.

**6. A quiet ritual.** Pick a daily five-minute moment: a slow brush, a lap of the yard, a few minutes on the porch. Dogs like patterns, and a small ritual becomes something to look forward to.

## Keep it practical

Use only things your dog already eats well, and keep xylitol, grapes, chocolate and other known hazards out of reach. Supervise anything made from household items, and rotate toys instead of leaving everything out. In a small apartment, work along a wall or in a hallway and keep the volume down for neighbors.

## Your dog, your pace

A young dog may want movement, while a senior may prefer a slow sniff and a nap. A nervous dog may enjoy novelty best in tiny doses. If your dog turns away, walks off or stops taking treats, that is a clear answer; make it easier or stop. If you have more than one dog, choose activities that suit every dog, and keep food games separate so nobody feels crowded.
