faqs:
  - q: What are the first signs a dog is overheating?
    a: AKC lists heavy panting, rapid breathing, and bright red gums and tongue as early signs, and pale or bluish gums and collapse as severe ones. Cornell's list includes drooling, vomiting, weakness, confusion and seizures. Heatstroke is an emergency, so call a vet rather than waiting to see.
  - q: Is it ever safe to leave a dog in a parked car for a few minutes?
    a: AKC says never leave a dog in a parked car, even for a short time, and Cornell says never leave a dog in a car unattended. Humane World says car temperatures can reach the 100s on a pleasant 70 degree day and that a running air conditioner is no guarantee. We found no source that gives a safe length of time.
  - q: Is it illegal to leave a dog in a hot car?
    a: In some states. The Animal Legal Defense Fund says that as of 2025, 32 states plus D.C. and Guam have some form of hot car law, and that the rules differ by state. Texas, for example, has no statute specific to dogs in parked vehicles, but a man was convicted under its general anti-cruelty law. Check the state you are in.
  - q: Do cooling vests and pads work?
    a: A small University of Florida study of seven working dogs found cooling vests helped some dogs cool faster, and a 2022 study in Animals also looked at vests on working dogs. We found no independent test of the Ruffwear vest or the Green Pet Shop pad, so their cooling wording is the makers' own.
  - q: How do I tell if the ground is too hot for my dog's paws?
    a: AKC says to hold your hand on the surface for ten seconds, and the Royal Kennel Club says seven seconds. Both say that if it is too hot for your hand it is too hot for your dog. The Royal Kennel Club adds that sun, wind and humidity change surface heat, so air temperature alone is a poor guide.
  - q: Is a cooling vest or a cooling pad better for travel?
    a: "A soaked vest is made for wearing on walks and the pad for resting on, and both makers' warnings apply: the pad should not be left in sun, heat or a hot car. Shade, water and rest come first."
  - q: How do I keep a dog cool at a rental without air conditioning?
    a: We found no source on safe indoor temperatures for dogs. Ask the host in writing whether the room the dog will sleep in is air conditioned and whether there is shaded outdoor space, and call a vet if the dog shows heat signs.

Keep the dog out of parked cars, walk in the early morning and evening, and carry water everywhere. Those three habits matter more than any gadget, and the one time to stop and call a vet is when you see the signs below.

## If your dog is overheating now

Early signs, per [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/): heavy panting, rapid breathing, bright red gums and tongue. Severe signs: pale or bluish gums, collapse. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) Cornell adds drooling, vomiting, weakness, confusion and seizures. [[2]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/heatstroke-medical-emergency)

1. Move the dog somewhere cool and ventilated, such as an air-conditioned space. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/)
2. Spray or sponge with tepid or cool water, never cold (AKC says extremely cold water can cause shock), and use a fan. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/)
3. Keep cooling on the way to a vet: wet the dog, aim the car's air conditioning at it and seek intensive care at a veterinary hospital. [[2]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/heatstroke-medical-emergency)
4. AKC says to go to a vet right away if the temperature reaches 104 F or will not come below 105 F in a reasonable time. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/)

The pages disagree on the exact heatstroke temperature (AKC's own pages give 105.8 F [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) and a 104/105/106 scale [[3]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/dog-paws-hot-pavement/); Cornell says 105 F or higher [[2]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/heatstroke-medical-emergency)). So call at the signs, not at a number. This page is not veterinary advice and cannot assess your dog. Find an emergency vet near the rental before you arrive; our [first-aid kit guide](/guides/dog-travel-first-aid-kit-guide) covers what to carry, including a thermometer.

## Which dogs are at higher risk

AKC names puppies, seniors, overweight dogs, dogs with health problems and short-snouted breeds. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) Cornell adds thick- or dark-coated dogs and dogs with respiratory or heart disorders. [[2]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/heatstroke-medical-emergency) If yours is on either list, ask your vet how much heat is too much. Our [senior dog rental guide](/guides/vacation-rental-with-a-senior-dog) and [puppy guide](/guides/puppy-at-a-vacation-rental) cover those trips.

## Never leave a dog in a parked car

AKC says never, even for a short time, because temperatures inside rise rapidly. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) Humane World says a car can spike into the 100s on a pleasant 70 degree day and a running air conditioner is no guarantee. [[7]](https://www.humaneworld.org/en/blog/heat-wave-wraps-around-us-take-steps) No source we read gives a safe number of minutes.

The law varies. The Animal Legal Defense Fund says that as of 2025, 32 states plus D.C. and Guam have some form of hot car law, and warns that a rescuer can still face property-damage or trespass charges, so call law enforcement first. [[8]](https://aldf.org/hotcars) Michigan State's Animal Legal & Historical Center keeps a [state-by-state table](https://www.animallaw.info/topic/table-state-laws-protect-animals-left-parked-vehicles); Texas, for example, has no dog-specific statute but one man was convicted under its general anti-cruelty law. [[9]](https://www.animallaw.info/topic/table-state-laws-protect-animals-left-parked-vehicles) Check each state on your route. The law is not the reason to stay with the dog; the heat is.

Plan the day so the dog is never alone in the car: take turns at stops, pick food stops with outdoor seating and do errands before you collect the dog. While driving, use the air conditioning, shade the car at stops and offer water often. Our [two large dogs in one car guide](/guides/two-large-dogs-in-one-car) covers heat stops on the road.

## Shade and surface heat

Use the hand test before every walk: hold your hand on the ground for ten seconds (AKC) or seven (the Royal Kennel Club). If it is too hot for your hand, it is too hot for paws. AKC cites JAMA data that asphalt reaches 135 F when the air is 86 F, and advises early-morning or evening walks on grass or in shade. [[3]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/dog-paws-hot-pavement/) The Royal Kennel Club adds that sun, wind and humidity change surface heat, so air temperature alone is a poor guide. [[4]](https://www.royalkennelclub.com/hotpavements) Cornell's prevention list adds frequent water and limiting hard exercise in hot, humid weather. [[2]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/heatstroke-medical-emergency)

Both pages describe pavement. Our suggestion: run the same test on sand, parking lots and rental deck boards.

## Water, the beach and the rental

**Hydration.** AKC says to bring enough water for your dog's size and activity and plan breaks. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) No source we read gives an amount in ounces, so go by your dog and the day. At the beach, Cornell advises half-hour breaks from the water, because a dog in and out of water all day can swallow a lot, and a good drink of fresh water before you go. [[13]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/summer-safety-tips) Our [life jacket guide](/guides/dog-life-jacket-for-lakes-and-beaches) covers saltwater, algae and beach rules. Bring shade (a pop-up umbrella is our suggestion) and skip the middle of the day.

**At the rental.** Ask the host in writing whether the air conditioning covers the room where the dog will sleep and whether there is shaded outdoor space. We found no source on safe indoor temperatures, so do not assume a closed-up house is cool. See our guides on [leaving a dog alone in a rental](/guides/can-you-leave-a-dog-alone-in-a-vacation-rental) and [pools and hot tubs](/guides/can-dogs-go-in-a-rental-pool-or-hot-tub).

**A bowl for the car.** Kurgo says the Splash Free Wander has a wedge shape that lays flat on a seat, a tapered lip to limit splashing, 24 oz capacity and food-grade silicone. [[14]](https://www.kurgo.com/p/splash-free-wander-dog-water-bowl/K01812) Use it at a stop with someone with the dog, never as a reason to leave water for a dog alone. Skip it if your dog drinks mainly on walks.

## Cooling vests and pads: what to look for

Water, shade and rest come first; AKC and Cornell both name cool water on the coat as first aid. [[1]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) [[2]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/heatstroke-medical-emergency) Vests and pads are optional extras, and the evidence for them is thin. The studies we found were small and on working dogs: a 2012 University of Florida study of seven dogs found vests helped some cool faster and called itself preliminary [[6]](https://www.vetmed.ufl.edu/2012/04/26/uf-study-looks-at-role-of-vests-in-heat-related-illness-of-working-dogs), and a 2022 military-dog study in *Animals* looked at vests in moderate heat. [[5]](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/12/18/2347) We found no independent test of any vest or pad sold for pet dogs, so treat cooling claims as the maker's.

- **Evaporative vest, for dogs on the move.** Look for chest-girth sizing and a size chart (measure the widest part of the rib cage). Ruffwear's Swamp Cooler is soaked, worn and re-soaked when dry, sized XXS (13-17 in) to XL (36-42 in), and the maker calls the cooling evaporative. [[10]](https://ruffwear.com/products/swamp-cooler-dog-cooling-vest) Its page gives two different between-sizes instructions, so read the chart for the size in front of you. Expect it to need a water source and to do less in humid air (our reasoning, not a sourced claim). Skip it if your dog mostly rests.
- **Gel pad, for a cooled room or car.** Look for a size matched to your dog stretched out, and read the warnings. The Green Pet Shop's Cool Pet Pad is pressure-activated, needs no water or electricity and cools up to 3 hours, per the maker, with a 15 to 20 minute recharge; it warns against direct sun, heat and hot cars, and against leaving it with a dog that chews or scratches. [[11]](https://www.thegreenpetshop.com/products/cool-pet-pad) Chewy lists four hours, so go by the maker's three. [[12]](https://www.chewy.com/education/dog/health-and-wellness/the-best-products-to-keep-dogs-cool-in-the-summer) The page also claims the pad prevents dangerous overheating; no test is cited, and no pad makes a parked car safe.

Neither replaces working air conditioning, shade or a vet's advice for a higher-risk dog.
