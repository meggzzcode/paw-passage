faqs:
  - q: Can I take my senior dog to a vacation rental?
    a: Often yes, if your vet agrees the trip suits your dog and the rental suits your dog's mobility. Age alone is not the test; call your vet, then check stairs, floors and house rules before booking.
  - q: Is a ground-floor rental best for a senior dog?
    a: A ground-floor listing can still have entrance steps or slippery floors, so ask for photos of the route from parking to the door, the threshold and the floors. Airbnb's step-free filters were built for people, so treat them as a starting point.
  - q: What should I ask a host about stairs and floors?
    a: Ask for photos of the route from the parking spot to the door, any steps, the bedroom path, and the flooring, and ask whether you may put down your own non-slip rug or ramp. A message template is in the guide.
  - q: Can I put a rug or ramp down in a rental?
    a: None of the manager pages we read say either way, so get the host's answer in writing before you move anything. Pick items that grip the floor and do not damage it.
  - q: What if my senior dog has an accident or leaks in the rental?
    a: Tell your vet first if leaking is new. For the rental, bring washable or waterproof layers, report and clean any accident, and settle how accidents are handled before you book; Brindley Beach says its pet fee does not cover unreported accidents.
  - q: How often should I stop on the drive?
    a: No vet source we read gives a number or a distance. Watch your dog for discomfort, ask your vet what suits your dog, and plan stops where someone can stay with the dog.

To rent a vacation home with a senior dog, check the stairs, the floors and the host's rules before you pay, and ask your vet first whether the trip suits your dog. Everything below is built around those three checks.

You booked the cabin with the view, and then you saw the photo of the staircase to the only bedroom. Your fourteen-year-old dog hesitates at two steps at home. Most senior-dog travel advice is about the car. This guide is about the rental, because a place you cannot change is where an older dog's trip is won or lost.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. Rules vary by place and host.

## Do this first

Talk to your vet, get photos and written answers from the host, and only then pay.

1. **Call your vet** before booking and ask whether the trip suits your dog. The sources we read set no age cutoff, so it comes down to your dog's health, mobility and comfort in the car.
2. **Send the host the message below** and request photos of the route, steps and floors.
3. **Pack for slipping and accidents:** non-slip rugs, your dog's own bed, washable layers and your vet's medication list.

Nothing here needs buying to book. What to buy, and in what order, is in [What to buy, in order](#what-to-buy-in-order) near the end.

Four portable fixes come up across vet sources: non-slip rugs or mats, short nails, grip aids, and ramps with traction. [[1]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs) [[2]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) [[3]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)

| Situation | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Your vet says the trip is fine and the dog moves comfortably | Go, and check the rental carefully |
| Your dog is stiff, slow to rise or hesitates at stairs | Call your vet first; these are listed signs of joint disease [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[3]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis) |
| Your dog is newly confused, restless at night or having accidents | Call your vet first (see the vet list near the end) |
| Your vet advises against travel | Consider a sitter or boarding |

## What should you check on the listing?

Stairs and floors decide most trips, and a listing rarely shows enough of either. Treat the listing text as a starting point and the photos as the answer.

- [ ] **Stairs.** Where is the bedroom, and how many flights lie between the entrance, bedroom and bathroom? Are the steps open-backed, narrow or steep, and are the treads bare or carpeted?
- [ ] **Floors.** Dog Aging Project says to limit an older dog's access to slippery hard floors, and Banfield suggests non-slip rugs or yoga mats on them. [[1]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs) [[2]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home)
- [ ] **Thresholds and door sills** at the entrance and to the yard.
- [ ] **Route from parking to the door.** Steps, curbs, a slope.
- [ ] **Bed and couch height.** Cornell lists trouble getting onto couches as a sign of joint disease and suggests ramps for beds, couches or cars. [[3]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- [ ] **Steps down to the yard,** and whether it is fenced (see our [fenced-yard guide](/guides/vacation-rental-secure-fenced-yard)).

**A borrowed photo standard.** Airbnb publishes how hosts should photograph step-free features: no steps, stairs or curbs over 2 inches along the entrance route, photos every 10 feet, thresholds shown close up. [[5]](https://www.airbnb.com/resources/hosting-homes/a/a-guide-to-photographing-accessibility-features-30) It also lists "Step-free access" and "Step-free bedroom access" as accessibility features guests can filter for. [[6]](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3138) These are built for human guests, so applying them to a dog is our inference, and the standard has no flooring or bed-height photos.

## What should you ask the host?

A vague "is it accessible?" gets a vague answer; "send a photo of the steps from the driveway" gets a fact. Request:

- The route from parking to the front door, with any steps or curbs
- The entrance threshold, close up
- The stairs to the bedroom and bathroom, from the side and the front, showing whether they are open-backed and what covers them
- The main-floor and bedroom flooring
- The path to the yard and its slope

A message you can copy:

> Hi [Host], we are considering [dates] with our [age]-year-old [breed]. [Name] is steady but slow on stairs and slippery floors. Could you tell me: (1) Is there a step-free route from parking to the door, and could you send a photo? (2) What flooring is in the living area and bedroom, and are the stairs carpeted or bare? (3) Are there stairs between the bedroom, bathroom and entrance? (4) Are there steps to the yard? (5) May we put down a rubber-backed rug or a small ramp, and bring our own bed and washable bedding? (6) How are accidents by an older dog handled, and is any deposit involved? We will clean up and tell you right away. Thank you.

If your dog may leak, say so in the message. Send it through the booking platform so the answers are on record. Our [pet-friendly rental checklist](/guides/pet-friendly-vacation-rental-checklist) covers the general questions, and our [pet fees guide](/guides/how-vacation-rental-pet-fees-work) explains how the total is built. For a young dog instead, see our [puppy rental guide](/guides/puppy-at-a-vacation-rental).

## How do you make slippery floors safer in a rental you cannot change?

Bring grip with you, put the question to the host before laying anything down, and expect hard floors.

- **Rugs or mats.** Banfield suggests non-slip rugs or yoga mats. [[2]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) Dog Aging Project suggests them where the dog lies down and in high-traffic areas, and says to be present the first time your dog uses them. [[1]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Short nails** help paw-pad traction. [[1]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Grip aids.** Boots, rubber-soled socks and toe grips can help; the same page cautions about toe grips for chewers. [[1]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Ramps and steps for furniture or a car.** AKC says to match the height to the sofa, bed or trunk, check the weight limit and look for a non-slip surface. [[13]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/general-health/dog-stairs-and-ramps/) None of these pages says a ramp works for a full indoor flight, so for rental stairs use the photo check and ask the host before you move anything.
- **Your own bed.** Banfield lists an orthopedic bed with a low profile, washable cover and non-slip bottom. [[2]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) A familiar bed also keeps the host's furniture clean. Our picks are in [What to buy, in order](#what-to-buy-in-order).

**What owners report (4 of 17 rental-related forum threads).** Rubber bath mats cut to fit the steps, traction strips and carpet laid on bare treads. [[14]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/looking-for-advice-on-wooden-steps/) [[15]](https://tripawds.com/forums/size-and-age-matters/senior-stair-struggles) [[16]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/stairs-was-fine-and-then-it-changed) [[17]](https://forums.mst3k.com/t/older-dogs-and-stairs/20432) Three of the four are from Tripawds, a forum about three-legged dogs, so they are not independent, and the advice matches the vet sources above.

## What if your dog balks at the stairs when you arrive?

Do not force it. Stop, work out a plan with the host, and call your vet if the refusal is new; hesitating at stairs is a listed sign of joint disease. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[3]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis) On Tripawds, an owner who pulled a refusing dog up by the leash was warned it "could definitely turn him off" the stairs, which is one report but a good reason to do the photo check before you pay. [[16]](https://tripawds.com/forums/hopping-around/stairs-was-fine-and-then-it-changed) Get the cancellation terms in writing in case the stairs turn out to be a problem.

## What if your dog leaks or has accidents?

Tell your vet first, since leaking in an older dog can have causes a vet should check. Then plan the rental side.

**What hosts and owners report (6 forum threads, a small non-random sample).** Hosts say guests have arrived with pads for geriatric dogs, keep a cleaner and old towels on hand, or ask guests to report any accident; owners bring their own blanket and washable bedding. [[18]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/instant-book-and-pets/45138) [[21]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/pet-friendly-hosts-tips-for-setting-up-your-place/18610) [[22]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/are-these-reasonable-rules-for-dogs/24289) [[23]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/uk/3413892-Question-about-a-holiday-cottage) [[24]](https://airhostsforum.com/t/renting-to-dog-owners-your-thoughts/3126?page=2) That lines up with Brindley Beach's page, where the pet fee does not cover unreported accidents. [[8]](https://www.brindleybeach.com/node/3691) These are views, not platform rules, and the incontinence threads we read [[19]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/5090152-old-dog-support-thread?page=1) [[20]](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/the_doghouse/1128953-geriatric-dog-peeing-in-house) are from Mumsnet, a UK forum.

What to do:

- **Layers.** Pack washable or waterproof bedding, pads and a sheet for furniture. Our pick for pads is in [What to buy, in order](#what-to-buy-in-order).
- **Report and clean right away.** Tell the host or manager what happened and what you did.

If a host later asks for money, our [damage-charge guide](/guides/airbnb-pet-damage-charge-after-checkout) covers the clocks and what to reply. Whether a deposit or charge applies to an accident you reported and cleaned is a question for the host in writing; our [pet fees guide](/guides/how-vacation-rental-pet-fees-work) explains how pet fees work.

## What if your senior dog gets seriously ill on the trip?

- **Save an emergency vet near the rental.** ASPCA says to save a local veterinary emergency hospital and its poison control number in your phone. [[25]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts) No source sets a distance, so pick the minutes you would accept, then check the map for the nearest clinic to the rental address and confirm its hours.
- **Save poison help.** ASPCA Animal Poison Control is 888-426-4435 [[26]](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control) and Pet Poison Helpline is 855-764-7661 [[27]](https://www.petpoisonhelpline.com/); a consultation fee may apply.
- **Decide who decides.** If someone else takes your dog to a clinic while you are away, the clinic will want to know who can approve treatment and how to reach them. Our suggestion, not a vet's: ask your vet what to write down for a carer, including medications and what you would and would not want done, keep your phone reachable, and agree on a spending limit in advance.

## What should you pack, and what about the drive?

ASPCA says to carry medical records if your dog's history is complicated, to put visible ID on the collar, consider a microchip, and check for an interstate health certificate. [[25]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts) Ask your vet and the destination state about certificate timing. Add a vet-written medication list (plus extra in case the return is delayed), a [first-aid kit](/guides/dog-travel-first-aid-kit-guide) and your dog's tags (see our [lost-dog guide](/guides/if-your-dog-gets-loose-on-a-trip)); the [cabin packing list](/guides/dog-friendly-cabin-packing-list) and [packing-list tool](/tools/packing-list) cover the rest.

How far can a senior dog travel in a car? No vet source we read gives a stop interval or a distance, so ask your vet what your dog can manage. In nine mostly UK forum threads and owner posts, three mention frequent stops or a slower pace and three mention shade or heat, with no agreed numbers. ASPCA says never to leave a pet in a parked car [[25]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts), so plan stops where someone can stay with your dog. See our [car safety guide](/guides/dog-car-safety-harness-and-crate-guide) for restraints.

## When should you call your vet?

Call before booking for any sign below, and again if it appears on the trip. This list is a prompt to call, not medical advice.

- **Joint signs.** Banfield and Cornell list stiffness, trouble rising, hesitation on stairs and difficulty getting onto couches, and Banfield says not to dismiss them as just old age. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[3]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- **Behavior changes.** ASPCA lists confusion and changes in sleep, activity or house training and says to report all changes to your vet. [[28]](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/behavior-problems-older-dogs) AKC says a full evaluation by your vet is imperative. [[29]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/recognizing-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
- **Dementia is not the only explanation.** Dog Aging Project says pain, dental disease, sensory loss and other illness can cause behavior changes. [[30]](https://dogagingproject.org/understanding-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
- **Routine.** The same page says older dogs have a much tougher time with inconsistent routines; keep feeding, walks and bedtime close to normal. [[30]](https://dogagingproject.org/understanding-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
