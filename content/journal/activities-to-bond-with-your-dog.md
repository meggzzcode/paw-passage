Bonding is not a project. It is what happens when your dog learns that time with you is reliably good. Most advice for it is simple, and most of it fits into the day you already have. You do not need special equipment, only a few minutes and a willingness to follow your dog's lead.

## The short answer

Bonding grows from shared, low-pressure moments where your dog has a choice: sniffing together, simple games, calm touch and time near each other. Short and regular beats long and rare.

## Six ideas

**1. Follow the nose.** Take a slow walk and let your dog pick the direction at a few turns. You are showing them that what they care about matters to you.

**2. Play a search game.** Hide a few small treats and let your dog work out where they are. Scent games let your dog succeed, and success is a great shared memory. ASPCA's guide describes starting simple and making it harder over time.

**3. Learn one trick together.** Pick something easy and keep each session to a few minutes. End on a success.

**4. Offer calm touch.** Slow strokes along the shoulder or a gentle brush suit many dogs. Stop for a moment and see if they lean in. If they move away, let them.

**5. Share a quiet spot.** Sit near your dog on the floor, with no phone and nothing to do. Dogs often relax most when you do.

**6. Make something small.** A pawprint card, a photo of a favorite spot or a note about their day becomes a keepsake you made together. Your dog's part is simply being there.

## Practical considerations

Give your dog an easy way to opt out, such as a bed or a corner of the room. Keep sessions short and stop while they are still interested. Use treats your dog already eats well. If a dog is new, shy or from a shelter, go slowly and expect the pace to be their own.

## Your dog, your pace

A new dog may need days or weeks of low-key company before they are ready for more. A senior may prefer gentle touch to games. A lively dog may like a search game most. Dogs show interest with soft eyes, a loose body and a willingness to come back for more. If your dog seems worried, ease off, and ask a veterinarian or qualified trainer about anything that concerns you.
