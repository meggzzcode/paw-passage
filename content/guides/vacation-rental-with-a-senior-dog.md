You booked the cabin with the view, and then you saw the photo of the staircase to the only bedroom. Your fourteen-year-old dog hesitates at two steps at home. Most senior-dog travel advice talks about the car; this guide is about the rental, because a place you cannot change is where an older dog's trip is won or lost.

This is general information, not veterinary advice. Rules vary by place and host.

## The short answer

- Ask your vet whether the trip suits your dog before you book. We found no age cutoff in the sources we read.
- Check the listing for stairs, floors, thresholds, bed and couch height and steps to the yard, and ask the host for photos. The check list is below.
- Four portable fixes come up across vet sources: non-slip rugs or mats, short nails, grip aids, and ramps with traction. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs) [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- Manager rules can clash with an older dog's needs, such as furniture rules, unattended rules and weight or count limits. Ask in writing.
- We found no vet source that gives a stop interval, a distance to a vet or a heat limit. Ask your vet for your dog's numbers.

## Can you take a senior dog to a vacation rental?

Often, yes, if your vet agrees the trip suits your dog and the rental suits your dog's mobility. We found no age rule in the sources we read, so the decision rests on your dog's health, mobility and car comfort, and your vet is the first stop, before the booking.

Forum threads show the dilemma, but they are anecdotes, not evidence. One owner on a forum described a nine-year-old Lab with hip dysplasia and a booked third-floor rental with six flights and no elevator; the only reply suggested boarding the dog, hiring a strong person to carry him, or asking about paying extra for a ground-floor unit. [[18]](https://thelabradorforum.com/posts/390587) We do not know what happened next. The lesson is about timing: the problem was found after booking.

| Situation | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Your vet says the trip is fine and the dog moves comfortably | Go, and check the rental carefully |
| Your dog is stiff, slow to rise or hesitates at stairs | Ask your vet first; these are listed signs of joint disease [[5]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis) |
| Your dog is newly confused, restless at night or having accidents | Ask your vet first; see the section on behavior changes |
| Your vet advises against travel | Consider a sitter or boarding, and ask what to look for |

## What should you check on the listing?

Check six things: stairs and floors, thresholds, the route from parking to the door, bed and couch height, steps to the yard, and quiet. A listing rarely states all of these, so the answer is usually photos and a message to the host, not the listing text.

- [ ] **Stairs.** Where is the bedroom, and how many flights between the entrance, the bedroom and the bathroom?
- [ ] **Floors.** Bare wood, tile or laminate give less grip than carpet. Dog Aging Project says to limit an older dog's access to slippery hard floors. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- [ ] **Thresholds and door sills** at the entrance and to the yard.
- [ ] **Route from where you park to the door.** Steps, curbs, a slope.
- [ ] **Bed and couch height.** Cornell lists trouble getting onto couches as a sign, and suggests ramps for beds, couches or cars. [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- [ ] **Steps down to the yard,** and whether it is fenced (see our [fenced-yard guide](/guides/vacation-rental-secure-fenced-yard)).
- [ ] **Quiet.** Ask about neighbors and road noise if your dog sleeps poorly in new places. We did not find a source that sets a standard, so this is your judgment.

### A borrowed photo standard

Airbnb has a published standard for how hosts photograph step-free features. It says to show no steps, stairs or curbs over 2 inches along the entrance route, with photos every 10 feet and thresholds shown close up. [[1]](https://www.airbnb.com/resources/hosting-homes/a/a-guide-to-photographing-accessibility-features-30) It also lists "Step-free access" and "Step-free bedroom access" as search filters. [[2]](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3138)

Important caveat: these are built for human guests. Whether they describe what is easy for a dog is our inference, not something Airbnb says. Step-free access to the entrance is plausibly relevant to a dog, but the standard has no flooring or bed-height photos. Use it as a model for what to ask for, not as a guarantee.

## What should you ask the host?

Ask for specific photos and written answers before you pay. Hosts are used to being asked; the point is that a vague "is it accessible?" gets a vague answer, while "send a photo of the steps from the driveway" gets a fact.

Photos to request:

- The route from parking to the front door, with any steps or curbs
- The entrance threshold, close up
- The stairs to the bedroom and bathroom, and the flooring on them
- The main floor flooring, and the bedroom floor
- The path to the yard and the yard's slope

A message you can copy:

> Hi [Host], we are considering [dates] with our [age]-year-old [breed]. She is steady but slow on stairs and slippery floors. Could you tell me: (1) Is there a step-free route from parking to the door, and could you send a photo? (2) What flooring is in the living area and bedroom, and are the stairs carpeted or bare? (3) Are there stairs between the bedroom, bathroom and entrance? (4) Are there steps to the yard? (5) May we put down a rubber-backed rug or a small ramp, and bring our own bed? (6) How are accidents by an older dog handled, and is any deposit involved? We will clean up and tell you right away. Thank you.

Send it through the booking platform so the answers are on record. Our [pet-friendly rental checklist](/guides/pet-friendly-vacation-rental-checklist) covers the general questions, and the [pet cost tool](/tools/pet-cost-check) helps you confirm the total.

## How do you make slippery floors safer in a rental you cannot change?

Four fixes appear across vet sources: non-slip rugs or mats, short nails, grip aids and ramps with traction. They are portable, so you can bring them, but ask the host before you move anything.

- **Rugs or mats.** Banfield suggests non-slip surfaces such as rugs or yoga mats. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) Dog Aging Project suggests them where the dog lies down and in high-traffic areas, and says to be present the first time your dog uses them. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Short nails.** Dog Aging Project says nail length matters for paw-pad traction. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Grip aids.** Boots, socks with rubber soles and toe grips can help; the same page cautions about toe grips for chewers. [[3]](https://dogagingproject.org/mobility-tips-and-tricks-for-senior-dogs)
- **Ramps and stairs for furniture or a car.** AKC says to match the height to the sofa, bed or trunk, check the weight limit, and look for a non-slip surface. [[7]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/general-health/dog-stairs-and-ramps/) Banfield says ramps need the right incline and traction. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) We found no authoritative slope number, and none of these pages says a ramp works for a full indoor flight of stairs.

Banfield also lists an orthopedic bed with a low profile, a washable cover and a non-slip bottom. [[4]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/care-for-older-pets/senior-proof-your-home) Bring your own bed so there is something familiar and the host's furniture stays untouched.

Do not assume you can carry a large dog up stairs. That was one forum reply, not a vet recommendation, and we found no vet source on it. It is also a safety question for you.

## How far should the rental be from a vet?

We could not confirm any distance. No source we read sets one. ASPCA says to save a local veterinary emergency hospital at your destination, and its poison control number, in your phone. [[11]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts)

A practical approach: decide how many minutes you would accept, then check on a map for the nearest emergency vet to the rental address, confirm its hours and whether it is open around the clock, and save the number before you leave. That is our suggestion, not a standard.

## What should you pack and carry?

ASPCA says to carry a copy of medical records if your dog's history is complicated, to use a collar with ID plus a microchip, and to check for an interstate health certificate. [[11]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts) We could not read AVMA's travel page, so we are not stating certificate time windows; ask your vet and the destination state.

- [ ] Records, and a medication list written by your vet
- [ ] Medications, with extra in case the return is delayed
- [ ] Collar with ID tag, and microchip details (see our [ID tag guide](/guides/dog-id-tags-and-gps-trackers-for-travel-guide))
- [ ] Your dog's own bed, non-slip rugs or mats, and any ramp
- [ ] Accident kit: towels, paper towels, waste bags and your own sheet or blanket for furniture
- [ ] The nearest emergency vet and its number saved offline
- [ ] A [first-aid kit](/guides/dog-travel-first-aid-kit-guide) and our [cabin packing list](/guides/dog-friendly-cabin-packing-list)

## What about the car and rest stops?

We found no vet source that gives a stop interval. Some forum replies and commercial blogs give numbers, but we could not trace them to a vet, so we are not repeating them. Ask your vet what suits your dog and watch your dog for discomfort.

**What most people do (level 2, small sample).** We read nine forum threads and owner posts, mostly UK and mostly not about rentals. Five of the nine (three forum threads and two owner posts) mention frequent stops or a slower pace, and three mention shade or heat as a constraint. The threads disagree on the details, and none gives an interval a vet backs, so treat this as a description of what owners say, not advice. The interval itself stays unknown: ask your vet.

On heat, we found no vet threshold either. ASPCA says never to leave a pet in a parked car. [[11]](https://www.aspca.org/news/traveling-your-pets-dos-and-donts) Some forum replies say a short wait is fine when it is cool; that is anecdote, and the ASPCA line is the safer one for an older dog. Plan stops where someone can stay with your dog, and see our [car safety guide](/guides/dog-car-safety-harness-and-crate-guide). Anything to calm your dog for the drive is a question for your vet.

## When should you ask your vet first?

Ask your vet before booking if your dog shows any sign below, and again if it appears during the trip. We do not give medical advice here; this list is a prompt to call.

- **Joint signs.** Banfield and Cornell list stiffness, trouble rising, hesitation on stairs and difficulty getting onto couches or beds, and Banfield says not to dismiss them as just old age. [[5]](https://www.banfield.com/Senior-hub/common-health-issues-in-senior-pets/osteoarthritis-in-dogs-cats) [[6]](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/osteoarthritis)
- **Behavior changes.** ASPCA lists confusion, changes in sleep, activity or house training and says to report all changes to your vet. [[10]](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/behavior-problems-older-dogs) AKC says a full evaluation by your vet is imperative. [[9]](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/recognizing-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
- **Do not assume dementia.** Dog Aging Project says pain, dental disease, sensory loss and other illness can cause behavior changes. [[8]](https://content.dogagingproject.org/understanding-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)
- **Routine.** The same page says older dogs adjust to change less easily, so keep feeding, walks and bedtime as close to normal as you can. [[8]](https://content.dogagingproject.org/understanding-behavioral-changes-in-senior-dogs/)

None of these is a reason to cancel. They are things to be mindful of, and each has a person who can answer. Save every written answer, with the date, in the booking thread or a note on your phone, so you can show it later. If a platform's rules are unclear, ask Airbnb or Vrbo support through the booking.
