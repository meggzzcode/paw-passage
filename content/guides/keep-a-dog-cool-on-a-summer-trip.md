faqs:
  - q: What are the first signs a dog is overheating?
    a: Heavy panting, rapid breathing and bright red gums and tongue are early signs; pale or bluish gums and collapse are severe. Heatstroke is an emergency, so call a vet rather than waiting to see.
  - q: Is it ever safe to leave a dog in a parked car for a few minutes?
    a: AKC says never leave a dog in a parked car, even for a short time, and Cornell says never leave a dog in a car unattended. Humane World says a car can reach the 100s on a pleasant 70 degree day.
  - q: Is it illegal to leave a dog in a hot car?
    a: In some states, and the rules differ by state; the parked-car section above has the Animal Legal Defense Fund count and examples. Check the state you are in.
  - q: Do cooling vests and pads work?
    a: A small University of Florida study of seven working dogs found cooling vests helped some dogs cool faster, and a 2022 study in Animals also looked at vests on working dogs. We found no independent test of the Ruffwear vest or the Green Pet Shop pad, so their cooling wording is the makers' own.
  - q: How do I tell if the ground is too hot for my dog's paws?
    a: AKC says to hold your hand on the surface for ten seconds, and the Royal Kennel Club says seven seconds. Both say that if it is too hot for your hand it is too hot for your dog. The Royal Kennel Club adds that sun, wind and humidity change surface heat, so air temperature alone is a poor guide.
  - q: Is a cooling vest or a cooling pad better for travel?
    a: "A soaked vest is made for wearing on walks and the pad for resting on, and both makers' warnings apply: the pad should not be left in sun, heat or a hot car. Shade, water and rest come first."
  - q: How do I keep a dog cool at a rental without air conditioning?
    a: We found no source on safe indoor temperatures for dogs. Ask the host in writing whether the room the dog will sleep in is air conditioned and whether there is shaded outdoor space, and call a vet if the dog shows heat signs.

## If your dog is overheating right now

1. **Get the dog out of the heat** to shade or air conditioning.
2. **Call a vet or an emergency vet now**, on the way if possible, and tell them the dog's temperature if you can take it.
3. **Cool the dog with cool (not cold) water.** Wet the dog's body, fan it, and offer water only if the dog is alert and able to drink; never force it.
4. **Do not use ice-cold water or ice baths.** [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) says extremely cold water can cause shock.
5. **Even if the dog seems better, a vet check is needed**.

AKC describes a temperature above 104 F as an emergency; Cornell uses 105 F. If you are unsure, treat it as an emergency. AKC recommends a rectal thermometer for a dog first-aid kit; ask your vet how to take a temperature.

Heavy panting, rapid breathing and bright red gums and tongue are early signs. Pale or bluish gums and collapse are severe, and Cornell adds drooling, vomiting, weakness, confusion and seizures. Find an emergency vet near the rental before you arrive; our [first-aid kit guide](/guides/dog-travel-first-aid-kit-guide) covers what to carry, including a thermometer.

Keep the dog out of parked cars, walk in the early morning and evening, and carry water everywhere. Those habits matter more than any gadget.

## Which dogs are at higher risk

Puppies, seniors, overweight dogs, short-snouted breeds, thick- or dark-coated dogs and dogs with health problems, including heart or breathing disorders ([AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/), Cornell). If yours is one of them, ask your vet how much heat is too much. Our [senior dog rental guide](/guides/vacation-rental-with-a-senior-dog) and [puppy guide](/guides/puppy-at-a-vacation-rental) cover those trips.

## Never leave a dog in a parked car

[AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) says never, even for a short time, because temperatures inside rise rapidly. Humane World says a car can spike into the 100s on a pleasant 70 degree day and a running air conditioner is no guarantee.

If you see a dog in a hot car, call law enforcement first. The [Animal Legal Defense Fund](https://aldf.org/hotcars) says that as of 2025, 32 states plus D.C. and Guam have some form of hot car law, and a rescuer can still face property-damage or trespass charges. Penalties and rescuer protections differ widely, so check the statute for each state on your route.

Plan the day so the dog is never alone in the car: take turns at stops, pick food stops with outdoor seating and do errands before you collect the dog. While driving, use the air conditioning, shade the car at stops and offer water often. Our [two large dogs guide](/guides/two-large-dogs-in-one-car) covers heat stops.

## Shade and surface heat

Use the hand test before every walk: hold your hand on the ground for ten seconds. If it is too hot for your hand, it is too hot for paws. [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/dog-paws-hot-pavement/) says the ground may be too hot once the air is 85 F or over without a chance to cool, and advises early-morning or evening walks on grass or in shade. Run the same test on sand and deck boards too. In hot, humid weather, give frequent water and limit hard exercise (Cornell).

## Water, the beach and the rental

**Hydration.** [AKC](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/heat-stroke-in-dogs/) says to bring enough water for your dog's size and activity and plan breaks. At the beach, Cornell advises half-hour breaks from the water, because a dog in and out of water all day can swallow a lot, and a good drink of fresh water before you go. Our [life jacket guide](/guides/dog-life-jacket-for-lakes-and-beaches) covers saltwater, algae and beach rules. Bring shade (a pop-up umbrella works) and skip the middle of the day.

**At the rental.** Ask the host in writing whether the air conditioning covers the room where the dog will sleep and whether there is shaded outdoor space. See our guides on [leaving a dog alone in a rental](/guides/can-you-leave-a-dog-alone-in-a-vacation-rental) and [pools and hot tubs](/guides/can-dogs-go-in-a-rental-pool-or-hot-tub).

## Cooling vests and pads: optional

We found no independent test of any vest or pad sold for pet dogs, so treat cooling claims as the maker's. The small studies we found were on working dogs.

**Evaporative vest, for dogs on the move.** Look for chest-girth sizing and a size chart (measure the widest part of the rib cage). [Ruffwear](https://ruffwear.com/products/swamp-cooler-dog-cooling-vest)'s Swamp Cooler is soaked, worn and re-soaked when dry, and its page gives two different between-sizes instructions, so read the chart for the size in front of you. Expect it to need a water source and to do less in humid air (our reasoning). Choose it if most of the dog's heat exposure is on foot; skip it if your dog mostly rests.

**Gel pad, for a cooled room or car.** Pick a size for your dog stretched out. The Cool Pet Pad is pressure-activated, needs no water or electricity and cools up to 3 hours, per the maker, with a 15 to 20 minute recharge. Keep it out of direct sun, heat, hot air and hot cars (put a towel or blanket on a car seat first), and away from a dog that chews or scratches. Its page also claims the pad prevents dangerous overheating; no test is cited, and no pad makes a parked car safe. Choose it if the dog spends hot evenings on a cooled floor; skip it for a dog that chews bedding.

## Before you go

Check the forecast for each day, plan walks for early and late, and find an emergency vet near the rental. Buy in this order:

1. **A bowl or bottle** that suits how your dog drinks: the flat car-seat bowl or the one-hand walking bottle.
2. **A vest** only if your trip is mostly on foot.
3. **A pad** only if your dog mostly rests, with its warnings read first.

Add a bowl and bottle to the [packing list tool](/tools/packing-list), and add air conditioning and shade to your [pre-booking checklist](/tools/checklist).
